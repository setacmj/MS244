## Canvas 349 — 311

[![Canvas 349](https://iiif.wellcomecollection.org/image/b19608652_MS_244_0349.JP2/full/600,/0/default.jpg)](https://iiif.wellcomecollection.org/image/b19608652_MS_244_0349.JP2/full/1400,/0/default.jpg)

*Page 311 (numbered '311' at top right and bottom center; running header 'Aqua vite' / '& Co[m]posita'): Left column opens with an English spiced aqua vitae receipt (continuing from p. 310), followed by a long Latin receipt for 'Aqua mirabilis' (three distillations: mater balsami, oleum balsami, balsamum artificiale, and their medicinal uses). Right column continues the uses of Aqua mirabilis, followed by a circular alchemical diagram ('ignis contra naturam' surrounded by quadrants A–D: 'marcasita solis mall[eata]', 'Succu[s] lunarie', 'Sulphur Rubrum', 'materia arge[n]tea'), a Latin recipe for 'Aurum potabile' ('Modo tibi ostendam...'), and Henry Dingley's English commentary identifying 'marcasita solis' as leaf gold and 'succus lunarie' as moonwort growing at Hodgebatch near Bromyard, Herefordshire, concluding 'I wrote ye latyne as I found itt in an olde Booke / the english is my owne'.*

## Canvas 366 — 338

[![Canvas 366](https://iiif.wellcomecollection.org/image/b19608652_MS_244_0366.JP2/full/600,/0/default.jpg)](https://iiif.wellcomecollection.org/image/b19608652_MS_244_0366.JP2/full/1400,/0/default.jpg)

*Page 338 (numbered '338' at top left): Left column opens with receipts for corns on the toes ('Cornes vpon the Tooes') using galbanum, mercury water, mercury sublimate, Lapis Infernalis, and houseleek; followed by receipts for broken bones ('ffor Broken Bones exelent') in English and Latin, featuring Geranium and Osteocolla ('Beinbrug' / 'lapis Beinbruge') with notes on its occurrence in the Palatinate near Speyer, Heidelberg, Jena, Silesia, and Darmstadt (marked by a manicule). Right column cites Thomas Erastus on 'lapis sabulosus', records a 1624 personal case in which the compiler's brother Christopher broke his leg and was healed using the plaster on p. 337 and Osteocolla powder, followed by an experiment for warts and corns ('in J. B. p. 8'), a searcloth for fractures ('Mr Wodgall'), and a strong lye bath and splinting method.*
